**Voiceover Script:**
"Data loss can happen unexpectedly. Hardware fails without warning. Cyber attacks increasingly target schools. Natural disasters like floods or fires damage physical servers. Even accidental deletion can happen.

Beyond disaster scenarios, you have legal and compliance obligations. FERPA, the Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act, requires schools to maintain accurate student records. Most states have specific requirements for how long you must retain educational records—typically 5 to 7 years after a student graduates.

Your school management system stores years of critical data: attendance records, grades, disciplinary documentation, parent communications, financial records, and more. Losing this data impacts students, families, and your legal standing.

Regular backups ensure that even if something goes wrong, you can recover your data within hours or days, not permanently. Exports allow you to archive data on your own terms—keeping records securely for compliance purposes.

The best strategy combines automatic cloud backups—which happen without any effort from you—with periodic manual exports that you store securely in your own systems."

### Step 1: Understanding Backup Options (1:45 - 2:45)

**Visual Cues:**
- Diagram showing backup types:
  - Automatic cloud backups (cloud icon with checkmark)
  - Incremental backups (partial data icon)
  - Full backups (complete data icon)
  - Point-in-time recovery (calendar/timeline icon)
- Show backup frequency options:
  - Hourly
  - Daily
  - Weekly
- Display storage location options:
  - Provider's cloud (vendor-managed)
  - Your cloud account (AWS, Google Cloud)
  - Local storage (on-premises)

**Voiceover Script:**
"Most school management systems offer automatic backup capabilities. These happen behind the scenes without any action from you.

Automatic backups typically run daily and are stored in secure cloud servers. Many systems offer point-in-time recovery, meaning you can restore your data to any point in time. If something went wrong on Tuesday afternoon, you can restore to Monday night's backup.

The frequency of backups varies by plan. Basic plans might offer daily backups. Premium plans might include hourly backups, giving you even finer granularity for recovery.

Backups can be full or incremental. A full backup copies everything. An incremental backup copies only what changed since the last backup. Incremental backups save storage space and time, while full backups ensure you have complete snapshots.

Where backups are stored matters. Some systems back up to the provider's cloud infrastructure. Others allow you to connect your own cloud storage account—like Amazon S3 or Google Cloud Storage. This gives you additional control and redundancy. Some schools also back up locally to on-premises servers as a third layer of protection.

The best approach combines all three: daily automatic cloud backups from your provider, backups to your own cloud account, and periodic local exports to external drives."

### Step 2: Accessing Backup Settings (2:45 - 3:30)

**Visual Cues:**

- Click "Backup" or "Data Management"
- Show backup settings interface with:
  - Automatic backup status (on/off toggle)
  - Last backup timestamp
  - Next scheduled backup
  - Backup frequency dropdown
  - Retention policy (how long to keep backups)
  - Storage location selection
  - Manual backup button

**Voiceover Script:**
"To access your backup settings, go to your admin dashboard and select 'Settings' or 'Administration.' Look for 'Backup,' 'Backups,' or 'Data Management.'

You'll see your current backup configuration. There's usually a toggle to enable or disable automatic backups. This should always be enabled unless you have another backup system in place.

The interface shows when your last backup occurred and when the next scheduled backup will run. You can see how frequently backups are occurring—daily, hourly, etc.

You can adjust the backup frequency here if needed. More frequent backups mean better recovery options but use more storage. Most schools do well with daily backups.

The retention policy controls how long the system keeps backup copies. Setting a 30-day retention means the system keeps the last 30 days of backups but deletes older ones. For schools with long record-retention requirements, you might want 90-day or longer retention.

If you've connected external storage—like your own cloud account or local network drive—select it here to ensure backups go to the right location.

Most importantly, you'll see a 'Manual Backup' button. Click this to create an immediate backup right now. This is useful before making major system changes or before exporting critical data."

"Beyond automatic backups, you can manually export data. This is useful for archiving, compliance, or transferring to another system.

From your admin dashboard, look for 'Export' or 'Data Export.' You'll see options for different data types.

Most systems let you export student records, attendance data, grades, financial records, and communications separately. This modularity is helpful—you might export just attendance for an audit, or just grades for a specific semester.

You can select the data you want to export and the date range. For example, 'Export all attendance for Fall 2024' or 'Export all student records created between January and June 2024.'

Choose your export format. CSV is the most universal format—it opens in Excel and can be imported to almost any other system. PDF is good for archiving documents in their final form. JSON is useful for technical integrations. Excel format (.xlsx) is convenient if you'll work with the data in spreadsheets.

Initiate the export. Depending on the amount of data, this might take a few seconds to several minutes. You'll see a progress bar showing the export status.

Once complete, the system provides a download link. Download the file to your local computer or network storage. Store it securely—it contains sensitive educational and financial information. Consider encrypting it or storing it on password-protected external drives."

### Step 4: Restoration and Recovery (4:45 - 5:30)

**Visual Cues:**
- Screen recording: Show backup/restore menu
- Display restore options:
  - Restore from automatic backup (with point-in-time selection)
  - Restore from manual backup
  - Restore from external storage
- Show date/time picker for selecting recovery point
- Display confirmation dialog with warning
- Show restoration progress
- Display success notification

**Voiceover Script:**
"Now, let's talk about what to do if you need to recover data. From the backup settings, look for a 'Restore' option.

The system shows available backups. You can select any backup from your retention period. If you want to restore to yesterday's state, select yesterday's backup. If you want to go back further, select an older backup.

The system will ask you to confirm the restoration. This is important—restoring a backup overwrites current data. If something happened today that you're trying to undo, restoring to yesterday means losing everything from today. Proceed carefully.

Before initiating a full restore, consider creating a manual export of your current data, just in case. Better to have a backup of today's data before you restore to yesterday's.

Restoration times vary depending on how much data you're recovering, but might take anywhere from a few minutes to an hour for a full restore. Plan restoration during off-hours when the system can be offline.

Once restoration completes, verify that your data looks correct. Spot-check a few records to ensure the restore was successful.

For individual record recovery—like a teacher accidentally deleted a grade entry—contact your system support. They can often recover single records from backups without doing a full system restore."

### Best Practices (5:30 - 5:50)

**Visual Cues:**
- Slide-based best practices
- Icons for each practice:
  - Calendar (regular backups)
  - Lock (security)
  - Document (documentation)
  - Test (testing)

**Voiceover Script:**
"Best practices for data protection:

One: Keep automatic backups enabled. Never disable them. Set them to run daily at minimum.

Two: Verify backups are working. Periodically check that backups are completing successfully. Look at the timestamp—if it hasn't updated, something's wrong.

Three: Test your restoration process. Once yearly, restore a backup to a test environment and verify the data is intact. Don't wait for an emergency to discover your backups don't work.

Four: Store manual exports securely. Keep them encrypted and in multiple locations. One copy on your local network isn't enough—have a copy in a secure offsite location.

Five: Document your backup strategy. Write down what you're backing up, how often, and where. This documentation helps your successors understand your system.

Finally, combine methods. Don't rely on only automatic backups or only manual exports. Use both together for maximum protection."
